## 1. The Professional Today

**Wendell Killette — Servant Leader, Agile Coach & Release Train Engineer**

I clear the path so teams can deliver. For more than 20 years, I've coached and mentored teams at organizations including Charles Schwab, USAA, LPL Financial, AT&T, American Airlines, Cigna, and the IRS — guiding work from backlog through release.

When a team is stuck — waiting on an answer, a resource, or access to the tools they need — I'm the one they call. I know the system, I know who to ask, and I follow through until the blocker is gone.

My focus is the same now as it has always been: becoming a better servant leader.

### Strengths

**Protecting my teams**
Sprint after sprint, someone tries to attach new work to a team's commitment, often going around the Scrum Master to do it. Every addition puts delivery at risk. I step in, hold the line on what the team committed to, and route new requests through the right process. That's how teams learn I have their backs, and it's how I earn their trust.

**Mentoring**
The demands on delivery teams never stop, and people are often afraid to ask for help. I remind them that we're a dev family: we draw strength from each other. Asking for help isn't failure; it's putting the delivery ahead of your ego. I coach people to take themselves out of the picture and do what's best for the team.

**Listening**
Listening is how I lead every day: in stand-ups, in reporting, and in coaching through issues. It matters most during production releases, when a lot is happening at once. Listening closely lets me spot when we need to call in another resource before it becomes a problem.

## 4. The Impact

### "It was the team's commitment." *(Protecting the team)*
After sprint planning, my manager wanted to pull a developer off to another effort. That developer was carrying a story critical to our sprint, and to the Program Increment behind it. Losing them would have changed the outcome of both. I told my manager no.

Later, he came back and told me he wished all of his Scrum Masters had that kind of strength. But it wasn't about my strength. It was about the commitment the team made, and my job was to protect it.

### "You can count on him." *(Mentoring)*
In every retrospective, I ask the team to give each other kudos. It started as a small habit, and it built a strong bond.

I also coach that even though you own your story, you rarely deliver it alone; there are almost always outside dependencies. Over time, the team started recognizing people beyond our own walls, telling each other, "If you need help, you can really count on him." I mentored them to see that delivery runs on relationships, and to build those relationships across the organization.

### "No one has ever done that for me." *(Listening)*
One week, a business analyst on my team was sick and struggling to keep up. I could hear the decline in their work as the week went on. Every day I checked in: How are you feeling? Is there anything I can do? Every day the answer was, "I'll be okay."

Release night came, and this BA had to test each module as it went into production. I stayed with them for four hours, monitoring alongside them and encouraging them in Teams the whole way: "Things are going well. Great job." They finished the release as if they were completely healthy.

The next day, they messaged me: "Can I call you?" When I answered, the first thing I heard was crying. They wanted to thank me for checking on them constantly, and said no one had ever done that for them before.

I told them I take my role seriously. As this team's servant leader, and as a matter of personal character, that's simply how I work.

### "Finding the right person before rollback." *(Listening during releases)*
During production releases, a lot happens at once, and listening closely is how I spot trouble early. Twice, I heard that we needed a database engineer on the call, and none was there. I went on the hunt, found the right person, and got them on the call. That kept us from rolling back the release.
